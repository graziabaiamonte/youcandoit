One senior engineer can only write so much code. One senior engineer who makes three other
engineers better has quadrupled their impact. That is why every career ladder above mid-level
lists "helps others grow" as an expectation, and why the stories collected on
[StaffEng](https://staffeng.com/guides/) keep coming back to growing the people around you. This
session separates three ways of helping people (mentoring, coaching, sponsoring), covers pair
programming and how to give feedback that lands, then you mentor someone or write a tutorial.

## Mentoring vs coaching vs sponsoring

Think of learning to cook. A **mentor** is the experienced cook who tells you, "salt the pasta
water more than you think". A **coach** asks, "what do you think went wrong with that sauce?" and
lets you work it out. A **sponsor** is the head chef who tells the restaurant owner, "she's ready
to run the kitchen on Saturdays". All three help; they are different jobs.

| | Mentoring | Coaching | Sponsoring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What you give | Advice, experience, answers | Questions, reflection | Opportunities, visibility, advocacy |
| Typical phrase | "Here's what I'd do, and why." | "What options do you see?" | "I recommend her to lead this project." |
| Works best when | The person lacks knowledge or context | The person has the knowledge but is stuck | The person is ready but not seen |
| Where it happens | 1:1s, code review, pairing | 1:1s | Rooms they aren't in |

**Mentoring** transfers knowledge. It is fast and efficient, but if overused it creates
dependence: people learn to ask you rather than to think.

**Coaching** builds the person's own problem-solving. Slower in the moment, much more durable.
Good coaching questions: "What have you tried?", "What would you do if I weren't here?", "What's
the smallest next step?", "How will you know it worked?".

**Sponsoring** is spending your reputation on someone else: putting their name forward for a
project, inviting them to present, crediting them publicly. It is the rarest of the three and often
the most career-changing for the receiver, especially for people from under-represented groups who
tend to be over-mentored and under-sponsored.

> [!TIP]
> Before helping, ask: "Do you want me to tell you what I'd do, or help you think it through?"
> It makes the mode explicit and respects the other person's time and goals.

  N[New to the topic] -->|mentor: explain| C[Has knowledge, gets stuck]
  C -->|coach: ask| R[Ready for more scope]
  R -->|sponsor: open doors| G[Grows into next level]

## Pair programming

**Pair programming** means two people working on the same code at the same time. The classic
roles:

- **Driver**: has the keyboard, thinks about the line being written.
- **Navigator**: watches, thinks about the bigger picture (the next step, edge cases, naming), and
  spots mistakes.

Swap roles often (every 15 to 30 minutes, or at every passing test in **ping-pong pairing**, where
one person writes a failing test and the other makes it pass).

Pairing is one of the best mentoring tools because it transfers *how* you work, not just *what*
you know: how you read an error, which file you open first, when you write a test, how you search
the codebase. These things never appear in documentation.

Rules for pairing with someone more junior:

- **Let them drive most of the time.** If the senior drives, the junior watches a performance. If
  the junior drives, they build the muscle memory.
- **Think aloud** when you navigate: "I'm wondering whether this handles an empty list" teaches
  more than "add a check for empty lists".
- **Tolerate small detours.** Let them try the approach you suspect won't work if the cost is ten
  minutes. Discovering it themselves sticks.
- **Take breaks.** Pairing is intense; two 90-minute sessions beat a full day.
- **Remote works fine**: share an editor (VS Code Live Share, JetBrains Code With Me) rather than
  only a screen, so the driver can switch without friction.

> [!WARNING]
> "Pairing" where the senior grabs the keyboard whenever things get interesting is not pairing.
> If you notice your hands reaching for the keyboard, ask a question instead.

## Giving feedback

Most of a senior engineer's feedback happens in **code review**. Google's
[Engineering Practices](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/) guide to code review is a good
model. Its standard: approve a change once it definitely improves the overall code health, even if
it isn't perfect. Its advice on writing comments:

- **Be kind.** Comment on the code, not the person. "This function is hard to follow" rather than
  "you made this confusing".
- **Explain why.** Not just "use a dict here" but "a dict makes this lookup O(1); with 10k items
  the list scan shows up in profiles".
- **Label severity.** Prefix optional suggestions with `Nit:` or `Optional:` so the author knows
  what blocks merging and what doesn't.
- **Balance direction and teaching.** Sometimes point out the problem and let the author find the
  fix; sometimes just give the fix. Choose deliberately.
- **Praise good things too.** Specific praise ("nice test for the timezone edge case") tells
  people what to keep doing.

Outside code review, a simple structure for harder conversations is **Situation, Behaviour,
Impact (SBI)**:

> [!EXAMPLE]
> "In yesterday's incident review (**situation**), you explained the cache invalidation timeline
> step by step with the graphs (**behaviour**). The product team told me afterwards it was the
> first time they understood why it took two hours, and they've stopped asking for a rewrite
> (**impact**). I'd love you to present the next one too."
>
> Constructive version: "In the planning meeting (**situation**), you committed to the migration
> by Friday before checking with the database team (**behaviour**). They had to drop other work and
> felt blindsided (**impact**). Next time, could we check dependencies before giving a date?"

More habits:

- **Give it soon.** Feedback two months later is archaeology.
- **Praise in public, correct in private.**
- **Ask before you advise** when it isn't urgent: "Can I share an observation about the demo?"
- **Ask for feedback yourself.** People give more honest feedback to someone who visibly asks for
  and acts on it.

## Practice: mentor a junior or write a tutorial

Choose one of the two paths. Both force you to make your knowledge explicit, which is when you
discover what you actually understand.

### Path A: mentor someone (four weeks)

1. **Find a mentee**: a junior colleague, someone from a bootcamp community, or a friend learning
   to code.
2. **Agree on a goal** in the first meeting: something concrete for four weeks ("ship their first
   PR to an open-source project", "understand how their team's API is deployed").
3. **Meet weekly for 30 to 45 minutes.** Let them set the agenda. Use at least one session for
   pairing on their real work, with them driving.
4. **Practise all three modes** at least once: answer a question directly (mentoring), help them
   reason through a decision with questions only (coaching), and find one opportunity to make
   their work visible (sponsoring: share their PR, recommend them for something).
5. **Ask for feedback** in week four: what helped most, what didn't. Write down what you learned
   about teaching.

### Path B: write a tutorial (one weekend)

1. **Pick a topic you learned recently** in this roadmap, for example "adding JWT login to
   FastAPI" or "how YouCanDoIt derives XP from completions". Recent learners remember what was
   confusing.
2. **Define the reader and the outcome**: "a junior developer who knows Python; by the end they
   will have a working endpoint".
3. **Write it as steps** with runnable code, like the practice sections in these lessons. Explain
   *why* at each step, not only *what*.
4. **Test it on a real person.** Watch someone follow it without help. Every place they get stuck
   is a bug in the tutorial.
5. **Fix and publish** it (a blog, a GitHub README, an internal wiki). It becomes portfolio
   material for 10.4.2.

> [!TIP]
> Whichever path you choose, keep a short log of questions people asked you. Recurring questions
> are signals of missing documentation, and fixing those is mentoring at scale.
